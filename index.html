The Problem

Why This Matters

The Chicago Park District manages over 600 parks and 270+ facilities across the city — yet accessible, free open gym times for youth basketball and other sports are scarce, inconsistently scheduled, and often require registration fees or membership. Young Chicagoans are leaving the city just to find a place to play.

Lack of accessible recreational space disproportionately affects youth who don't have access to private gyms or clubs. Students from Chicago's North and South sides regularly travel to suburbs like Niles, Skokie, or Evanston simply to find an available gym — a commute that no young person should have to make in a city with hundreds of public facilities.

Accessible public sports keeps young people safe, active, and connected to their communities. This is a public health issue, a safety issue, and an equity issue — and the Chicago Park District has both the power and the responsibility to fix it.

Take Action → Vote

Your Vote Shapes Your Parks

The Chicago Park District is overseen by a Board of Commissioners appointed by the Mayor. This means your vote for Mayor and City Council directly influences park funding, hours, and policy. Local elections often have less than 20% voter turnout — which means your vote carries enormous weight.

How to Make Your Vote Count for Parks

When you turn 18, register to vote and show up for Chicago municipal elections. Research which mayoral and aldermanic candidates have concrete plans for park funding and youth recreation access — then hold them accountable.

📋 Register at vote.org 🗓 Chicago elections every 4 years 📍 Find your polling place at chicago.gov/vote 🔍 Research candidates' park funding positions 📣 Encourage friends and family to vote too

Not 18 yet? You still have a voice. Attend Park District public meetings, advocate on social media, petition your alderperson, and organize in your community — and when you turn 18, vote like parks depend on it, because they do.

Write to a Representative

Email Your Alderperson

Your alderperson represents your ward in Chicago City Council and has direct influence over park budgets and priorities. Find yours at chicago.gov, then copy the template below, fill in the [brackets], and send it.

Subject: Expanding Open Gym Access in Chicago Parks


Dear [Alderperson's Name],


My name is [Your Name], and I am a resident of Ward [Your Ward Number]. I am writing to urge you to advocate for expanded open gym access at Chicago Park District facilities, especially for youth basketball and recreational sports.


As a young person in Chicago, I and many of my peers regularly travel outside the city — as far as Niles — simply to find an available gym. This is not acceptable when our city manages over 600 parks. The lack of free, consistent open gym hours means many young Chicagoans are denied access to safe recreational spaces in their own neighborhoods.


I am asking you to support the following: (1) Increase the number of free open gym hours at Park District facilities across all wards; (2) Require the Park District to publish and maintain an up-to-date, searchable schedule of open gym times online; and (3) Allocate funding to keep gyms open on evenings and weekends for youth drop-in use at no cost.


Accessible recreation is not a luxury — it is a public health and safety issue. I would welcome the opportunity to speak with your office further about this. Thank you for your time and your service to our ward.


Sincerely,
[Your Full Name]
[Your Address and Zip Code]
[Your Email / Phone Number]

Sign & Share

Online Petitions

Petitions show elected officials that a large number of constituents care about an issue. Signatures collected online and delivered in person to a city council meeting or Park District board meeting carry real weight. Here's how to make it happen.

01

Find existing petitions on Change.org

Search "Chicago Park District equity" or "Chicago open gym access" on Change.org to find and sign active petitions targeting the mayor's office and city council. Share widely — numbers matter.

02

Create your own petition

Start a petition on Change.org or MoveOn.org addressed directly to the Chicago Park District Board of Commissioners and your alderperson. Collect signatures from classmates, teammates, and neighbors, then deliver them at a board meeting for maximum impact.

03

Apply social media pressure

Post about your experience — having to leave Chicago to find a gym — and tag your alderperson and @ChiParkDistrict on Instagram, X, or TikTok. Public pressure from young people has moved Chicago officials before. Use hashtags like #OpenCourtsChicago and #ChiParks.

Show Up

Protest & Civic Engagement

Beyond writing letters, here are concrete, actionable ways to show up and advocate for this issue in person. Showing your face — and bringing others — sends a clear signal to decision makers.

Attend a Chicago Park District Board Meeting

The Park District Board of Commissioners meets publicly and accepts public comment. Show up, sign up to speak for 2 minutes, and share your story. Meeting schedules are posted at chicagoparkdistrict.com. Bring a group — multiple voices are louder than one.

Speak at a City Council Budget Hearing

Chicago City Council holds budget hearings every fall. The parks budget is a line item — showing up to speak puts your issue on the public record in front of all 50 alderpersons and gets covered by local press. You don't need an appointment.

Organize a "Court Day" Rally

Organize a visible demonstration at a local park — invite peers to show up and play, display signs demanding open courts, and invite local journalists. A peaceful, visible show of demand proves the community wants this and creates shareable content that spreads the message further.

Write to Block Club Chicago or the Tribune

Local journalists cover Park District policy regularly. Reach out to Block Club Chicago with your story, or submit a letter to the editor of the Chicago Tribune. Community stories drive policy coverage and reach audiences beyond social media.

Your Constitutional Rights

The Constitution Backs You Up

The U.S. Constitution provides multiple foundations for the fight for equitable park and recreation access in Chicago. Here are three key provisions and how they directly tie into this issue.

14th Amendment Equal Protection Clause

The Equal Protection Clause prohibits states and their subdivisions — including city agencies like the Chicago Park District — from denying any person equal protection under the law. When wealthier or predominantly white neighborhoods receive more consistent open gym access, better-maintained courts, and longer operating hours than lower-income or minority-majority neighborhoods, this disparity can constitute unequal treatment by a public agency. Advocates can invoke the 14th Amendment to demand that the Park District demonstrate equitable distribution of recreational resources across all wards and zip codes, regardless of neighborhood income or racial composition. Federal courts have used the Equal Protection Clause in cases involving unequal public services, making this a real legal lever — not just a rhetorical one. When we argue that every Chicago neighborhood deserves equal access to public gyms and courts, we are making a 14th Amendment argument at its core.

1st Amendment Right to Petition & Assemble

The First Amendment guarantees citizens the right to peacefully assemble and to petition the government for a redress of grievances. This directly empowers every action on this guide: attending board meetings, organizing rallies at parks, circulating petitions, and writing letters to elected officials are all constitutionally protected acts. Young people under 18 hold these rights too — age does not restrict First Amendment protections. The government cannot prohibit peaceful civic action aimed at changing public policy, meaning your voice — whether spoken at a city council meeting, posted online, or expressed in a rally at a park — is protected at the highest level of law in the country. Showing up and speaking out is not just strategy; it is your constitutional right.

Preamble Promote the General Welfare

The Preamble to the Constitution establishes that one core purpose of government is to "promote the general welfare" of its citizens. Public parks, open gyms, and recreational facilities are a direct expression of this principle — they serve the physical and mental health of the community, reduce youth violence, and support social cohesion across neighborhoods. When public recreation infrastructure is defunded, inaccessibly scheduled, or inequitably distributed, the government is arguably failing its foundational obligation to promote the welfare of all residents — not just those who can afford private facilities. This framing is especially powerful when advocating to state or federal representatives for park funding, where the general welfare clause has historically justified broad government investment in public infrastructure.
